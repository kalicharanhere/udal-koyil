இசைத் தூண்கள் · Musical Pillars, Nellaiappar← Udal Koyil
What the legend says. In the Nellaiappar temple at Tirunelveli, slender stone colonettes are carved in clusters around main pillars, each cluster from a single block of stone. Tap one and it rings; tap its neighbour and the note changes, so each pillar is said to hold a swara. The pillars are traditionally attributed to the Pandya period, around the 7th century CE. Similar musical pillars stand at the Meenakshi temple in Madurai and at Suchindram, and, outside Tamil Nadu, at the Vittala temple in Hampi. Today they are often shared as stone that sings for reasons no one understands.
What the physics shows. Each colonette is a stone beam fixed at both ends, to the base and to the capital. Struck, it bends back and forth, and its lowest bending mode sets the note. For an idealised round rod clamped at both ends, f ≈ 3.56 · (d/4) / L² · √(E/ρ). For granite, with E about 50 GPa and ρ about 2,700 kg/m³, √(E/ρ) is about 4,300 m/s. So a thicker or shorter colonette sounds higher and a longer one lower: double the length and the note drops two octaves. The next mode sits about 2.76 times higher, not at a whole-number harmonic, which is part of why struck stone has a bell-like clink rather than a string's tone. Real colonettes are carved, tapered and irregular, and granite's stiffness varies widely, so real notes depend on the exact shape and stone; this model is idealised. Nothing here is a mystery. The honest wonder is the craft: choosing sound stone, sizing each colonette, and tuning it by ear by carving material away.

Legend: temple descriptions of the Nellaiappar temple, Tirunelveli (Tamil Nadu HR&CE); the Pandya date is a traditional attribution. Physics: Euler–Bernoulli beam theory for a beam clamped at both ends (first two modes βL ≈ 4.730 and 7.853); granite E roughly 10–70 GPa and ρ about 2,600–2,800 kg/m³ from standard material tables. Colonette sizes and notes in the model are illustrative, not measured at the temple.
